It's at the bottom of this page. Download it and keep it — save it to your phone, or print it. If you lose it, come back here and sign in again and you can fetch it as many times as you need.

Ringing up a sale

Every sale goes through the Square terminal as a product, and products come in two parts:

  • The item is the style. Five of them cover almost everything we make: infinity scarves, sash belts, half circle veils, rectangle veils and triangle fringe scarves. The four yarns are items too — Heavenly, Homespun, Artisan and Noble.
  • The variation is the colorway — the actual colour of the thing in the customer's hand. There are a lot of these: the yarns alone run to about forty colorways each.

The variation is the part that matters, and it is the part that's easy to get wrong. Every scarf under one item rings up at the same price, so a wrong colorway looks completely fine on the Square terminal and balances at the end of the day. What it quietly does is take one off the count of a colour that's still on the table and leave one on the count of a colour that's gone. Nobody finds out until we're making more of something we already have and none of what sold out.

The variations are listed in alphabetical order by colour name, so you can find the one you want by reading rather than by hunting. If you can't tell which colorway a scarf is, don't guess — that is exactly what the look-up book is for.

The look-up books

There's a look-up book at the booth, and it's printed two ways. Both halves cover the same scarves — the difference is how they're sorted, and which question each one answers quickly.

  • By colorway. A page per colour, with a photo and the barcodes of every style we dye in it. Use this when you have a scarf in your hand and need to know what it is.
  • By colour. The same pages, sorted into rainbow sections. Use this when a customer asks "what have you got in green?" — which is what they actually ask.

Every page has a coloured tab down the right-hand edge. Fan the stack with your thumb and the sections show from the side, so you can get to red without reading a single page. A scarf that's two colours is printed in both sections on purpose — it's genuinely in both, so don't be surprised to see it twice.

Learning the names

The book is the fallback, and there's nothing wrong with using it all season. But the fastest way to stop needing it is the two games on the public site — they're built for customers, and they work just as well for getting the colorway names into your head:

  • The matching game — pairs of scarves to match by sight.
  • The name quiz — a photo and four names, pick the right one.

Ten minutes of the quiz on a quiet morning is worth a lot of page flipping later.

Sending a photo in

There's one page for sending photos from the booth: pick your name, type your PIN, take the picture, send. Before it sends, it asks what the photo is for, and the answer decides what happens to it. There are two reasons, and they're unrelated jobs that share a page because there's only ever one moment when a phone comes out at a stall.

Something to share

The table looking good, a scarf in decent light, someone wearing one.

Permission is two separate questions

This is the bit worth reading twice, because it's about other people rather than about the app.

  • Your tick is your permission — that you're happy for the photo to be used.
  • It is not the permission of whoever is in it. If there's a recognisable person in the shot, you have to actually ask them, and then say on the form that you asked and they said yes.

Website and Instagram are separate ticks, because they're separate answers. One is a shop page; the other is a feed with an audience and a comment box, and people really do say yes to one and no to the other. Ask which, don't assume both.

And sending a photo with nothing ticked is a perfectly good answer. "Here, your call" is allowed and it still sends — you're never stuck choosing between over-promising on someone's behalf and not sending the picture at all.

A colorway nobody could identify

Sometimes something sells and nobody can say which one it was. The tag is gone, or it's a colorway none of us recognise. It's the same problem whether it's a scarf or a skein of yarn — the question is only ever "which colorway is this?"

Check the book first; that's what it's for. But if it sells and you still couldn't name it, don't just let it go. Send a photo with that reason picked, and add:

  • Roughly when it sold — near enough is fine.
  • The first six characters of the barcode, if any of the tag is still readable. That's the style, and it narrows a few hundred products down to a few dozen. Leave it blank if you can't read it.

The sale itself goes through Square perfectly normally — that's not what's broken. What's missing is which colorway actually left the table, so our count for that colour stays wrong until somebody works it out. A photo taken at the time is the only thing that makes that possible. A week later there is nothing left to go on.

Send it even if you're not sure it's worth sending. One that turns out to be nothing takes a single click to clear at this end; one that never gets sent leaves a number quietly wrong for the rest of the season.

Reporting your hours

You report your own hours, on your own phone, on the hours page. Pick your name, type your PIN, choose the day and how long you worked.

There are QR codes around the booth that open that page. Point your camera at one — there's nothing to look up, bookmark or remember, and you don't need the link from anybody.

  • The pay week runs Saturday to Friday, not Monday to Sunday. It's set up that way on purpose: a faire weekend falls inside one pay week, so the Saturday and the Sunday you work always land on the same timesheet instead of being split across two.
  • Hours go in quarter-hours off a dropdown — 6.25, 6.5, 6.75. There's no clocking in or out and no start time to fill in, so round to the nearest quarter and move on.
  • Booth hours during faire days only. Helping out with dyeing or prep back at the shop doesn't go here.
  • Report it the same day if you can. Reporting the same day twice is treated as a correction, not a second shift, so it's safe to fix a number you got wrong — it'll ask you before it replaces the old one.

Long days and long weeks get flagged for review, and a flag is not a complaint or an error. A thirteen-hour day at a festival is a normal thing that happens; the flag just means a person looks at the week before it goes to payroll. Put down what you worked.

Your name and your PIN

Your PIN is four digits and it isn't a secret — it stops the wrong name getting tapped, and that's the whole job. If you forget it, ask and it'll be read back to you.

These pages remember you on the phone you use, so after the first time your name and PIN are already filled in. That's built on the assumption that everyone uses their own phone. If you're ever holding somebody else's, use the "not you?" link before you type anything, or your hours will go in under their name.

If something's wrong

Tell Michael. A mistake mentioned the same day is a two-minute fix; the same mistake found six weeks later means unpicking everything that happened after it. Nobody is in trouble for a wrong tap — the only expensive version is the one nobody hears about.
